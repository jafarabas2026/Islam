Honouring Parents in Islam

In Islam, devotion to parents (Birr al-Walidayn) is not treated as optional cultural politeness. The Quran places it immediately after worshipping Allah alone, and the Prophetic traditions rank it among the highest acts of worship—even ahead of going out for Jihad—with duties that continue long after parents pass away.

Spiritual Rank
Second Only to Tawhid

Commanded immediately after worshipping Allah alone (Al-Isra 17:23) and second only to timely Salah (Sahih Bukhari 5970).

Maternal Priority
3× Before the Father

The Prophet repeated “Your mother” three consecutive times before naming the father (Biharul Anwar 74:49).

Continuity of Duty
Open Beyond Death

Du’a, charity, settling debts, and honouring their friends keep raising their rank in Paradise (Muslim 1631, 2552).

Section 05 Boundaries & Mercy

Disobedience (Uqooq) Is a Major Sin—Yet Repentance Never Closes

The Prophet sat upright to warn against Uqooq al-Walidayn, placing it right beside Shirk. Yet even if someone deeply regrets mistreating their parents—or realizes it after their parents have passed away—Islamic theology insists on hope, never despair.

Click to Expand — What Counts as Uqooq & Its Warnings

Ibn Hajar defined mistreatment of parents as any word or deed causing them hurt without legitimate religious justification (Fath al-Bari 10/406). Imam as-Sadiq explained that uttering “uff” is the mildest form of ill conduct, and that casting a hard, stern gaze—or a look of hatred even if parents were unfair—can prevent a person’s prayers from being accepted (Al-Kafi 4:50).

Imam Ali (a.s.) stated directly: “One who causes his parents to become sad has indeed committed Uqooq against them” (Biharul Anwar 74:64). Imam as-Sadiq also warned that whoever argues disrespectfully with their father suffers humiliation and disgrace (Biharul Anwar 74:71).

The Messenger of Allah warned that three sins have their punishment hastened in this life rather than deferred solely to the Hereafter: mistreatment/disownment of parents, oppressing people, and ingratitude toward kindness (Biharul Anwar 74:74). Imam as-Sadiq described Uqooq as a grave sin that “darkens the skies.”

A dying youth was unable to utter the Shahadah until his mother—whom he had alienated for six years—forgave him at the Prophet’s request (Biharul Anwar 74:75). Other narrations warn that the fragrance of Paradise, detectable from a thousand years’ distance, will not reach one who severed ties with parents (Biharul Anwar 74:62, 74).
